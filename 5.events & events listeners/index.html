The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) under construction at Brookhaven National Laboratory utilizes an advanced Rapid Cycling Synchrotron (RCS) to act as the highly specialized electron injector for the collider complex. To probe the internal 3D structure of protons and nuclei with unprecedented precision, the EIC demands high beam currents, luminosity, and an exceptionally high (~70%) level of beam polarization. The newly designed, 3.8-kilometer RCS is built to be completely "spin transparent"; its high periodicity configuration allows it to accelerate polarized electron bunches from 400 MeV up to operational energies of 5, 10, or 18 GeV in just 100 to 200 milliseconds without losing this crucial spin alignment. However, circulating such high-intensity electron beams at relativistic speeds generates massive amounts of synchrotron radiation (SR)—up to 10 megawatts of power—which presents a dual challenge. While the synchrotron mechanism effectively pushes particles to collision-ready energies, the resulting high-flux X-ray background requires a highly optimized, low-impedance vacuum chamber and absorber system alongside advanced Geant4-based simulation frameworks (like SynradG4) to shield the delicate ePIC spectrometer at the primary interaction point.
